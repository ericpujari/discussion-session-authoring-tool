# Ask or Guess? (Sample Session)

## Title

- Ask or Guess?

## Parent blurb

Some people believe if you want something, you should just ask for it directly. Others believe the people who care about you should notice what you need without being told. Neither approach is right or wrong — each one protects something different. This week, students explore six scenarios that test the line between asking and hoping to be understood. No right or wrong answers here — just interesting conversations with kids from around the world.

**Florence**

In some families, if you want something, you just come out and ask for it. In other families, you're expected to notice what someone needs without being told, and offer it before they ever have to ask. Both styles protect something real: one protects clarity, the other protects comfort. Today, you'll see six scenarios that test this exact tension. In a moment, you'll meet your team.

### Scenario 1: The Birthday Party 2-6

Your friend had a birthday party last weekend, and you didn't get an invitation. You're not sure if it was an accident, or on purpose. What do you do?

1. Ask directly: Ask them why you weren't invited — a real answer clears up the guessing, whatever it turns out to be.

[icon: raised hand]

2. Let it be: Say nothing and go on as normal — some questions aren't worth the risk of an awkward answer, especially if the friendship still feels fine.

[icon: closed door]

`challenge questions`

Is it possible to feel better without ever knowing the real reason?

Could asking make things more awkward, even if the answer turns out to be innocent?

Is staying quiet a form of trust, or a form of avoidance?

Would your answer change if this kept happening with the same friend?

`reflection question`

If you never brought it up, do you think you'd still be thinking about it a month from now?

### Scenario 2: Stuck on Homework 6-10

You're stuck on a hard homework problem, and your older sibling is in the next room doing their own thing. What do you do?

1. Ask directly: Knock and ask for help — it's clear, and it respects their time instead of making them guess what you need.

[icon: open door]

2. Try it alone first: Attempt the problem yourself before asking anyone — it builds your own skills and saves the ask for when you truly need it.

[icon: pencil and paper]

`challenge questions`

Is there a “right” amount of time to try something alone before asking for help?

If your sibling says no, does that mean they don't care about you?

Would your answer change if it was a parent instead of a sibling?

Could figuring it out alone feel more rewarding than getting the answer quickly?

`reflection question`

Have you ever misjudged whether someone wanted to be asked for help, or left alone?

### Scenario 3: Sabotage or Misunderstanding? 10-14

Your team agreed on a plan, but one teammate keeps doing something different. It's slowing your team down. What do you do?

1. Ask them about it: Ask what they're trying to do — maybe they misread the plan, or noticed something the rest of the team missed.

[icon: question mark]

2. Trust and wait: Say nothing for now and trust they have a reason — jumping to conclusions before the game plays out might cause more damage than their choice ever could.

[icon: hourglass]

`challenge questions`

What's the risk of assuming the worst, if you turn out to be wrong?

What's the risk of staying quiet, if they really are going off-plan on purpose?

Could asking about it sound like an accusation, even if you don't mean it that way?

How much time would you give someone before deciding to say something?

`reflection question`

Have you ever been accused of not following a plan, when really you just saw it differently?

### Halftime 14-15

Halftime. Time to check in.

So far, has your own default been more Ask, or more Guess?

- I usually ask directly.
- I usually hint or wait.

`reflection`

There's no right or wrong style here. But knowing your own default is the first step. In the second half, we raise the stakes.

### Scenario 4: The Deadline 15-19

You're overwhelmed this week, and your project is due tomorrow. Your teacher has given extensions before, but has never said outright that they're open to requests. How should you handle it, and why?

1. Ask directly: Email your teacher and ask for one more day — it's honest, and it gives them full information to make a fair call.

[icon: envelope]

2. Push through: Stay up late and finish on time — meeting the deadline means your teacher never has to decide whether to bend a rule for you.

[icon: alarm clock]

`challenge questions`

What's the upside of asking, beyond just getting more time?

What's the upside of not asking, even if it means more work for you?

Does asking for help ever look like weakness? Should it?

Would this be different in a strict classroom versus a relaxed one?

`reflection question`

Have you ever been surprised by how someone reacted when you finally asked them for something?

### Scenario 5: The Family Gathering 19-23

You're tired and don't want to attend a long family gathering this weekend, but your family has never directly said that attendance is required. What do you do?

1. Ask directly: Ask your parents if you can skip it — it's honest, and gives them the full picture so they can decide fairly.

[icon: raised hand]

2. Just go: Attend anyway — some things matter enough to family that you'd rather not make anyone decide whether your absence is “allowed.”

[icon: group of people]

`challenge questions`

In your family, would skipping a gathering be seen as rude? Or totally fine?

Would asking directly feel disrespectful in your family, or completely normal?

Does guessing put more pressure on the other person to read your mind? Or does it protect them from having to say no out loud?

Would your answer change if the gathering was for something huge, like a wedding?

`reflection question`

Do you think your family leans more toward Asking, or Guessing? What about your country?

### Scenario 6: Quiet on the Call 23-27

During your Synthesis session, one of your teammates has their camera off and hasn't said a word in a while. You're not sure if something's wrong, or if they're just listening quietly. What do you do?

1. Ask them directly: Ask if they're doing okay, or if they want to jump in — it's a small interruption that could make a real difference.

[icon: raised hand]

2. Assume they have their own reason: Assume they have a reason for staying quiet, and let them rejoin when they're ready — everyone participates differently.

[icon: headphones]

`challenge questions`

Could asking directly make someone feel put on the spot, even if you mean well?

Could leaving them alone make them feel unnoticed, even if that's not what they want?

Is there a difference between being “quiet” and “not participating”?

What would you want a teammate to do if you went quiet?

`reflection question`

Has anyone on your team gone quiet today? What do you think it means?

### Reflection 27-28

After six rounds of Ask vs. Guess, which do you think leads to fewer misunderstandings overall?

- Asking directly, even if it feels awkward.
- Guessing or hinting, even if it's less certain.

`reflection`

Neither style is “better” everywhere. But knowing which one you're using — and which one the other person is expecting — might be the real skill.
